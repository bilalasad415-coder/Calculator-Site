What Counts as Debt in a DTI Calculation

DTI includes recurring debt obligations — mortgage or rent, car loans, student loans, minimum credit card payments, and personal loans — but not everyday living expenses like groceries, utilities, or insurance premiums that aren't tied to a debt. This distinction sometimes surprises people, since it means DTI can look favorable even for someone with tight monthly cash flow, if most of their spending goes to non-debt expenses.

Front-End vs. Back-End Ratio

The front-end ratio measures housing costs alone against income. The back-end ratio (the one most commonly called "DTI") measures all debt payments, including housing, against income. Mortgage lenders typically look at both, since a low back-end ratio with an unusually high front-end ratio can still signal a stretched budget.

DTI Ranges and What They Typically Mean

DTI RatioGeneral Rating
36% or belowGenerally favorable to most lenders
37% - 43%Acceptable for many loan programs, may need compensating factors
44% - 49%Higher risk; fewer loan programs available
50%+Difficult to qualify for most conventional loans

Conventional mortgages often cap DTI around 43-45%, though government-backed loan programs (FHA, VA) sometimes allow higher ratios for qualified borrowers, particularly with strong credit or significant cash reserves.

Why DTI Matters Beyond Mortgage Approval

A high DTI doesn't just affect loan approval odds — it's also a practical signal of how much financial flexibility a household has if income drops or an unexpected expense arises. Many financial planners recommend keeping DTI meaningfully below lender maximums, not just low enough to qualify, as a cushion against financial shocks.
