How Conception Date Estimates Work

Conception can't usually be pinpointed to an exact day without fertility tracking, since it depends on when ovulation occurred and when sperm fertilized the egg — which can happen up to five days after intercourse, since sperm can survive that long in the reproductive tract. Most calculators instead estimate a likely window based on average cycle timing.

The Two-Week Estimate

In an average 28-day cycle, ovulation typically occurs around day 14, roughly two weeks after the last period starts. This is why "date of last period plus 14 days" is the most common quick estimate for likely conception, though it shifts earlier or later for shorter or longer cycles.

Why These Estimates Matter

Conception date estimates are sometimes used for legal or medical purposes — such as estimating gestational age when the last period date is uncertain, or for genetic and prenatal testing timelines that are scheduled relative to conception rather than the last period. They're also commonly used simply out of curiosity to pinpoint roughly when a pregnancy began.

Fertile Window vs. Exact Conception Day

The fertile window — the days when pregnancy is possible — spans roughly five days before ovulation through the day of ovulation itself, due to how long sperm can survive. This means conception could technically have occurred on any of several days, not just the single "day 14" estimate, which is why these calculators produce an estimate rather than a certainty.

Conception Date vs. Due Date Accuracy

Due dates calculated from the last menstrual period assume a standard 28-day cycle and ovulation on day 14 — assumptions that don't hold for everyone. An early ultrasound (before 14 weeks) is generally the most accurate way to date a pregnancy and, by extension, to estimate the conception window, since it measures the embryo or fetus directly rather than relying on cycle assumptions.
